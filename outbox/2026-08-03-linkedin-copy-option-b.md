$110,204 against two lawyers, and the claims dismissed with prejudice.

That is the largest penalty in a database of 1,822 court decisions dealing with hallucinated AI content, counted at its 1 August snapshot. 913 of them landed in the first seven months of 2026. The live page reads 1,825 today.

I downloaded the CSV from Damien Charlotin's database and did my own counting.

310 decisions record money. Most orders are small, and the median dollar sanction this year is $1,525.

The tail is where firms get hit. One order put $31,100 on two law firms jointly.

126 decisions carry a professional sanction such as a bar or disciplinary referral.

That is the part no fine covers. The opinion stays public and searchable long after the money is paid.

Then the engineering detail. In about 89% of the dated decisions, no AI tool is named.

Courts inferred the use from the output, or the record just says unidentified.

When a vendor is named it usually disputes that its product produced the error, and nothing in the record can settle it.

Unverified output plus missing provenance is the whole failure. Both halves are fixable in the pipeline:

- Citations bound to retrieval.
- An existence check on the document that leaves, not in the chat.
- Quoted text matched against the source.
- Model, prompt and retrieved IDs stored with the output.

The same shape shows up outside law. Last week a security team audited 55 vulnerability advisories from one account and could not reproduce 54 of them.
